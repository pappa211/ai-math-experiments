## 1. The order and first weight-two witness theorem is correct

For integers T,s >= 1, write B=10^T and k=B+1. Then

    ord_(k^s)(10) = 2T k^(s-1),
    min { S(N) : N>0, k^s divides N } = 2,

and the smallest positive multiple attaining weight two is

    N = 10^(T k^(s-1)) + 1.

I checked the potentially delicate points independently:

* The order modulo k is 2T. It divides 2T; a smaller order would be a proper divisor of 2T, hence at most T, contradicting 0 < 10^d-1 < k.
* Every prime p dividing k is odd. Since gcd(B-1,B+1)=1, v_p(10^(2T)-1)=v_p(k). Odd-prime lifting gives v_p(10^(2Th)-1)=v_p(k)+v_p(h). Requiring this to be at least s v_p(k) for every such p is exactly k^(s-1) dividing h. No primality or squarefreeness hypothesis on k is missing.
* With H=T k^(s-1), the exponent k^(s-1) is odd. The corresponding plus-sign valuation identity proves 10^H=-1 modulo k^s. Since the cyclic subgroup generated by 10 has order 2H, all exponents giving -1 are H modulo 2H.
* A decimal integer of weight two is either 2·10^a or 10^a+10^b with a>b. The first cannot be divisible by k^s, which is odd, coprime to 10, and at least 11. The second requires a-b>=H, with equality and b=0 attaining the smallest value. Weight one is impossible for the same coprimality reason.

For s>=2 its length H+1 is at least k+1. Thus weight two is excluded within the at-most-k-digit span, while it is always attained without that span bound. This is a real length-sensitive statement for a fixed small weight; it is not the required statement for weight s.

This improves the local diagnosis over Session 01's compatible-residue constructions: it uses the full growing modulus k^s, not merely a fixed or moderately growing modulus. Its implication is negative for unrestricted minimum-weight methods. It does not prohibit a new theorem controlling both weight and span.

## 2. The weight-ten specialization lemma is correct and sharp

If P is a polynomial with nonnegative integer coefficients, P(1)<=10, and 10^T+1 divides P(10), then X^T+1 divides P in Z[X].

Indeed, reduction by X^T=-1 gives a remainder R of degree less than T with coefficient l1 norm at most 10. Then

    |R(10)| <= 10·10^(T-1) = 10^T < 10^T+1.

Its evaluation is divisible by 10^T+1, so R(10)=0. A nonzero integer polynomial of degree r>=1, leading coefficient of absolute value a, and coefficient l1 norm at most ten has lower terms of total evaluated magnitude at most (10-a)10^(r-1), strictly smaller than a10^r. It cannot vanish at ten; a nonzero constant cannot either. Therefore R=0. Evaluating the factorization at one also proves that P(1) is even.

The threshold is sharp even for decimal digit polynomials:

    P(X)=X^(2T)+X^(T+1)+9,
    P(10)=(10^T+1)(10^T+9),
    P(1)=11.

For T>=2 the remainder is 10-X, a nonzero polynomial vanishing at ten. For T=1 the two high monomials coincide and the remainder is 11; P=2X^2+9 is still a valid decimal digit polynomial. Both cases really violate the proposed conclusion at weight eleven.

This is a valid numerical-to-symbolic implication under a small coefficient budget, unlike simply assuming symbolic divisibility from numerical divisibility. It is distinct from Session 01's fixed-polynomial separation argument. However, the remaining fixed-point exponent/digit sum is at least 2501, and this lemma only treats weight at most ten. It currently excludes no new relevant candidate.

### A further exact obstruction to multiplicity lifting

The first theorem itself gives a particularly direct warning. For every s>=2, let

    P_s(X)=X^(T k^(s-1))+1.

Then P_s has weight two, k^s divides P_s(10), and X^T+1 divides P_s. Nevertheless **(X^T+1)^2 does not divide P_s**: every complex root of X^H+1 is simple, since its derivative H X^(H-1) is nonzero at every root. Thus numerical divisibility to arbitrarily high power does not imply corresponding polynomial multiplicity even at weight two. This example exceeds the allowed span; it does not refute a carefully formulated span-sensitive multiplicity statement.

An iteration of the weight-ten lemma would also need to control the quotient's coefficients. They need not remain nonnegative: X^(3T)+1=(X^T+1)(X^(2T)-X^T+1). A proof cannot silently reuse the original positivity budget after division.

## 3. The coding formulation is exact, but not a reduced problem yet

A fixed point supplies a decimal digit polynomial D of degree k-1, D(1)=s, and D(10)=k^s. Searching for short positive-weight relations modulo k^s is a faithful necessary-condition formulation. On the length curve, however, any positive multiple of k^s with at most k decimal digits has multiplier at most nine, because k^s>=10^(k-1). A lower bound exceeding s for every such multiple would be stronger than excluding equality for the power itself. No estimate establishing that stronger claim was found.

A residue automaton has k^s residue states before adding a length restriction. Its exactness does not yield a symbolic compression or a uniform bound. The cited [FUN paper, Section 7 and Theorem 15](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/opus/volltexte/2020/12771/pdf/LIPIcs-FUN-2021-10.pdf) does give a binary linear-in-modulus sturdiness test; I reopened the primary text. It does not give the growing-modulus, restricted-span estimate needed here.

The generalized-abc diagnostic is also correctly qualified. I independently checked [Hölzl–Kleine–Stephan, Conjectures 1.2, 1.4 and 1.8](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/881532D8B69924BBFA5C76A32A52063A/S1446788725000084a.pdf/improved_lower_bounds_for_strong_nconjectures.pdf): the total-gcd-one version predicts quality 2n-5; the pairwise-coprime variants have different hypotheses. Splitting decimal digits into s unit terms gives n=s+1 and coefficient 2s-3, which does not contradict a height k^s using the generic radical bound. Keeping m occupied positions gives coefficient 2m-3, and neither m<s/2 nor uniform dimension-dependent constants follow. This rejection concerns the stated substitution, not every conceivable use of abc.
